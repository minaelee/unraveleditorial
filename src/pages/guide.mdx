        <Heading level="h2" id="goal">what's your goal?</Heading>

        The type of editorial service you need depends on your goals. Do you plan to self-publish as an indie author, or are you aiming at traditional publishing? 

        If you're **self-publishing**, the typical editorial path is: developmental editing, line editing, and copy editing, followed by proofreading. 

        If you're seeking **traditional publishing**, consider that any publisher that purchases your manuscript will run it through their own editorial process. Thus, I recommend you focus on making your manuscript strong enough to attract a literary agent, rather than aiming for a fully polished final version. Unless you have significant copy issues, skip the copy editing and proofreading and focus on polishing the developmental and line aspects. The quality of your story and the quality of your prose is what will grab an agent's attention.
    
        If you're not sure what your manuscript needs, I recommend starting with a manuscript assessment.

      <Heading level="h2" id="types">understand the types of editing</Heading>

      Note that separate editors can be used for each <Link href="/services">service</Link>. Each stage can also be repeated if needed.
    
      <Heading level="h3" id="types-dev">the large brush: manuscript assessment or developmental editing</Heading>
      <p>
        In my other life, <Link href="https://minaelee.com/art">I'm a painter</Link>. My painting process typically starts with a sketch, followed by broad strokes using a large brush. When I've gone as far as I can with the large brush, I switch to successively smaller brushes until I've reached my desired level of refinement.
      </p>
      <p>
        Think of this editing stage as the large brush. This stage analyzes the big-picture elements — what's working and what's not, what's missing, what needs to be moved around. For fiction, this includes plot, pacing, character development, world building, and more. For nonfiction, the focus is on structure, organization, clarity, and how well the material serves its intended reader. The editor for this stage not only identifies issues but provides guidance to resolve them.

        It's important to complete this stage before moving on to the "smaller brushes" of line and copy editing because it addresses the most fundamental story issues. This prevents you from wasting time refining a sentence that might get dropped for developmental issues later.

        Depending on your needs, you can choose a manuscript assessment (also known as a manuscript evaluation or critique) or a full developmental edit (sometimes called a structural, substantive, or content edit), which includes specific in-manuscript guidance.

      </p>

        My **manuscript assessment** includes:
        <ul class="list-disc pl-6 space-y-0">
          <li>a multi-page editorial letter (also known as a revision letter) summarizing the main issues and revision guidance</li>
          <li>30-minute follow-up call after you receive the letter</li>
        </ul>
    
        I also offer a **light** version of the manuscript assessment that focuses on the top three critical issues. This version includes the 30-minute follow-up call.
      <p>
        My **developmental edit** includes:
        <ul class="list-disc pl-6 space-y-0">
          <li>the same multi-page editorial letter as the full manuscript assessment</li>
          <li>detailed notes within the manuscript to point out specific developmental issues and advise on how to resolve them</li>
          <li>60-minute follow-up call after you receive the edit</li>
        </ul>

        What it does **not** include:
        <ul class="list-disc pl-6 space-y-0">
          <li>This is not a line-level or copy edit. I will not be providing detailed sentence-level revisions. I will not be fixing typos, grammar, or punctuation errors. I will be looking at the bones of the story itself, not the details of the way you tell it. However, I will let you know generally if I see significant line or copy issues throughout your manuscript.</li>
        </ul>
      </p>

      <Heading level="h3" id="types-line">the medium brush: line editing</Heading>
      <p>
        This is the stage that addresses sentence-level clarity, flow, and style. I'll provide detailed inline feedback regarding sentences that can be improved qualitatively. Some common issues tackled in **line editing** include:
        <ul class="list-disc pl-6 space-y-0">
          <li>awkward or unclear sentences</li>
          <li>repetitive phrasing</li>
          <li>weak transitions</li>
          <li>consistency and continuity issues</li>
          <li>perspective and point-of-view problems</li>
          <li>confusing sequences</li>
        </ul>
    
        The line edit should enhance your prose while respecting your unique authorial voice.
      </p>

      <Heading level="h3" id="types-copy">the fine brush: copy editing</Heading>
      <p>
        **Copy editing** is performed after developmental and line editing. This stage focuses on ensuring the manuscript is correct, consistent, and adheres to style guidelines.
    
        I'll provide:
        <ul class="list-disc pl-6 space-y-0">
          <li>a customized style guide for consistency, including the narrative timeline, character details, and other key elements of the manuscript. I use an adapted version of the style guide taught in <Link href="https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/C/bo183690033.html">The Chicago Guide to Copyediting Fiction</Link>, customized for your manuscript.</li>
          <li>inline corrections and suggestions for consistency, continuity, grammar, spelling, punctuation, and usage throughout the manuscript.</li>
        </ul>

        Note that line and copy editing can overlap in practice. For example, both pay attention to continuity and consistency issues. However, they differ in focus: the line editor is concerned with quality, style, and narrative flow, while the copy editor is primarily concerned with correctness and adherence to style guidelines.
      </p>

      <Heading level="h3" id="types-proof">final checks: proofreading</Heading>
      <p>
        **Proofreading** is the last stage of the editorial process. The proofreader looks for any remaining errors in the final proof before publication, such as typos, grammar and punctuation mistakes, and formatting issues. I do not provide this service, but I highly recommend that you employ one if you are self-publishing.
      </p>
