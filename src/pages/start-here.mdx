      <Heading level="h2">assumptions</Heading>

      Before we begin, I want to state a couple of assumptions:

      - Your manuscript is completed and ready for editorial feedback.
      - It is not a brain-dump or first-draft manuscript, and you have edited it yourself to the best of your ability.

      If your manuscript needs significant work to be ready for editing, you might need to seek out a book doctor or writing coach first.

      <Heading level="h2">what does your completed, ready-to-edit manuscript need next?</Heading>
      
      <Heading level="h3">the large brush: developmental editing</Heading>
      <p>
        In my other life, <Link href="https://minaelee.com/art">I'm a painter</Link>. My painting process typically starts with a sketch, followed by broad strokes using a large brush. When I've gone as far as I can with the large brush, I switch to successively smaller brushes until I've reached my desired level of refinement.
      </p>
      <p>
        In editing, think of the developmental editing stage as the large brush. At this stage, I'll analyze the big-picture elements — what's working and what's not, what's missing, what needs to be moved around. For fiction, this includes plot, pacing, character development, world building, and more. For nonfiction, I'll focus on structure, organization, clarity, and how well the material serves its intended reader.
        
        It's important to complete this stage before moving on to the "smaller brushes" of line and copy editing because it addresses the most fundamental issues. This prevents you from wasting time refining a sentence that might get dropped for developmental issues later.
      </p>

      <p>
        A **developmental edit** includes:

        - detailed notes within the manuscript to point out specific issues and advise on how to resolve them
        - a multi-page editorial letter summarizing the major issues and recommendations

        If you do not have the budget for a full developmental edit, consider the lower-priced **manuscript assessment**. The manuscript assessment includes the editorial letter only, without detailed in-manuscript guidance.
      </p>

      <Heading level="h3">the medium brush: line editing</Heading>
      <p>
        This is the stage that addresses developmental and prose issues at the sentence level. I'll provide detailed inline feedback regarding sentences that can be improved qualitatively. Some common issues tacked in **line editing** include:

        - awkward or unclear sentences
        - repetitive phrasing
        - weak transitions
        - consistency and continuity issues
        - perspective and point-of-view problems
        - confusing sequences
    
        I'll do my best to enhance your prose while preserving and respecting your unique authorial voice.
      </p>

      <Heading level="h3">the fine brush: copy editing</Heading>
      <p>
        **Copy editing** is performed after developmental and line editing. In this stage, I'll focus on ensuring the manuscript is free of egregious errors and the prose is polished and consistent.
    
        I'll provide:
        - a customized style guide for consistency, including the narrative timeline, character details, and other key elements of the manuscript. I use an adapted version of the style guide taught in <Link href="https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/C/bo183690033.html">The Chicago Guide to Copyediting Fiction</Link>.
        - corrections for consistency, continuity, grammar, spelling, punctuation, and usage throughout the manuscript.

        Note that line and copy editing can overlap in practice. For example, both pay attention to continuity and consistency issues. However, they differ in focus: the line editor is concerned with quality, style, and narrative flow, while the copy editor is primarily concerned with correctness and adherence to style guidelines.

      </p>

      <Heading level="h3">proofreading</Heading>
      <p>
        There's one final step before the manuscript goes to print: proofreading. Proofreaders focus on catching any remaining errors in the final proof, such as typos and formatting issues. I do not offer proofreading, but I highly recommend it.
      </p>

      <Heading level="h2">how the services fit together</Heading>

      Note that separate editors can be used for each service. Each stage can also be repeated as necessary; a manuscript might require more than one round of developmental editing, for example.

      <ol class="list-decimal pl-6 space-y-0">
        <li><strong>Develop:</strong> An editor provides a round of developmental editing that tackles big-picture issues and guidance on how to resolve them. This includes an editorial letter and detailed inline notes within the manuscript. Alternatively, for a manuscript assessment, the editor provides the editorial letter only, and the author decides how to implement the recommendations themselves.</li>
        <li><strong>Revise:</strong> The author reviews the assessment or edit and implements changes to the manuscript (or "stets" them, meaning they decline to accept them).</li>
        <li><strong>Line edit:</strong> Once the content and structure are stable, an editor provides guidance on revising the prose.</li>
        <li><strong>Revise:</strong> The author reviews the line edits and implements changes as desired</li>
        <li><strong>Copy edit:</strong> An editor copyedits the manuscript for correctness and consistency.</li>
        <li><strong>Revise:</strong> The author reviews the copy edits and implements changes as desired.</li>
        <li><strong>Proofread:</strong> A proofreader reviews the final proof for any remaining errors, such as typos and formatting issues.</li>
      </ol>
